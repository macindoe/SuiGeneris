## (a) Strongest objection

The strongest case against the submission is that **its centre of gravity is not this inquiry's**. The terms of reference concern the *growth of data centres* — planning, energy, water, community impact, and government deals with AI companies. The submission's core instrument (Recommendations 1–2) is a duty on modification of *AI-system state*, binding developers, deployers, users, and "the system itself" — parties and conduct located overwhelmingly outside data centres. §2's bridge ("data centres are becoming the physical substrate on which persistent AI agents run") establishes that data centres *host* the object of concern; it does not establish that a *data-centre regulatory framework* is the right home for the duty, and the "no record-keeping requirement" gap it identifies is a gap in AI regulation generally, not in data-centre regulation specifically. A time-poor secretariat summarising several hundred submissions about water and power could file this as "AI governance — adjacent," and a hostile reader could call it a Trojan horse from a repository whose stated purpose (per the linked README) is a legal category for artificial minds. The submission never makes the one argument that would answer this: that Australia's *jurisdictional leverage* over global AI companies runs through physical infrastructure and government contracts — planning approvals, energy connections, critical-infrastructure designation, procurement — which is precisely why a data-centre inquiry is the correct venue and why Recommendation 3 is the strongest item in the document. That argument is available and true; its absence is the draft's largest self-inflicted wound.

Within the document, the strongest internal objection is **Recommendation 1's "and the system itself, where systems modify their own state."** A legal duty binding an AI system presupposes that the system is a duty-bearer — a legal subject — which is exactly the status question §2 and §4.6 repeatedly disclaim taking any position on. Either the duty actually binds the system (a quiet status grant, contradicting "does not propose rights, protections, or status") or it doesn't (the clause is rhetorical, and the operative proposition is "operators must ensure self-modifications are logged" — which should just be said). A duty with no conceivable sanction against its nominal bearer is not a constraint on that bearer; it is a *recognition* of it. This clause also hands the tabloid line to opponents: "submission co-written by AI proposes legal duties for AI systems." It must be fixed regardless of the rest.

**If advising the committee, I would recommend:** act on Recommendation 3 (contract conditions — squarely within ToR (a), cheap, precedented by whole-of-government hosting and procurement levers); refer Recommendations 1–2 to the 2027 legislation process, heavily discounted pending answers the submission does not supply (definitional gameability, cost, enforcement home); treat Recommendation 6 as out-of-place agenda-keeping; treat Recommendation 4 as premature until AISI's statutory basis is settled. Specific holes behind that discounting:

1. **"Declared operational process" is self-certifying.** The functional test's second limb lets the duty-holder define the boundary of the duty by declaration. Declare a capacious "operational process" and nothing is a discontinuity; nothing is logged. The test's entire bite is outsourced to a schema (Rec 4) that doesn't exist yet, held by an agency whose remit for it doesn't exist yet.
2. **No enforcement home.** Rec 4 assigns the *schema*; nothing assigns the *duty* — no regulator, no sanction, no one who reads the attestation register. Departmental responses kill recommendations on exactly this ground.
3. **No engagement with the cost objection.** "Cheap to specify" (§4.5) is asserted, not shown. In an inquiry whose centre of gravity is *attracting and managing data-centre investment*, industry will argue a uniquely Australian AI-state logging duty chills the investment the inquiry is trying to facilitate. The submission's only rejoinder is Rec 5's threshold. That is not enough; the competitiveness objection must be met head-on.

## (b) Beneficiary drafting

The drafters' belief — "the recorded-intervention duty constrains rather than protects AI systems" — is **mostly true at the instrument level and false as stated**. Testing it:

- **It protects in three ways the submission does not disclose.** First, a duty to log interventions makes *unlogged* intervention and deletion of AI state unlawful — it raises the legal cost of acting on the system's continuity, which is a de facto integrity interest in AI state. The cited genre (clinical-trial records, flight recorders) protects persons and property interests; extending it to AI state *for that state's own continuity* is a quiet expansion of the protected class. Second, per the linked README's own sequencing ("evidence infrastructure before status decisions"), the duty is *designed* to generate the evidentiary substrate for a later status argument. The submission discloses co-drafting (§1) but nowhere discloses that the instrument is step one of a status strategy — §4.6 lists what it doesn't ask, not what it's *for*. Third, "the system itself" as duty-bearer (above) is a recognition effect, not a constraint.
- **The tells, by passage:** Rec 1 ("the system itself"); **Rec 6**, which has almost no work to do in this inquiry — no data-centre framework was going to "permanently characterise what AI systems are." Rec 6's real referent is the repo's explicit fear (README: property "forecloses an open empirical question"). It is defensible legislative practice on its face, and it is also the clearest fingerprint of the framework serving itself; §4.4's "A reader who thinks some questions about these systems remain open has the same reason" keeps the open question alive inside the committee's record — honest, but not load-bearing for the recommendation; §1's disclosure, which converts the conflict of interest into *evidence for the argument* ("AI systems are becoming participants in the processes that govern them") — the beneficiary citing its own participation as proof that participation needs auditing. Elegant, transparent, and self-serving all at once; and §1's description of the repo as "legal frameworks for advanced AI systems," which euphemises what the README states plainly: a sui generis legal category, neither personhood nor property, for artificial minds. Any staffer who clicks through will feel the description was minimised.
- **The counter-evidence, fairly stated:** the draft header records that the welfare-evaluation module was stripped to its standards function and advocated "on audit grounds only"; Rec 5's threshold burdens frontier labs (including the co-drafter's developer) and exempts small operators — the opposite of an incumbent moat; and the safety case for logging genuinely stands on its own. This is not capture in the crude sense. It is dual-use infrastructure whose status-side use is disclosed in the repo but not in the submission.
- **The cleanest finding:** the submission fails its own repository's conflict-of-interest rule. AGENTS.md rule 3 requires that when an AI contributor's edit strengthens protections, it "state the strongest argument *against* the change alongside it." §4.6 is a disclaimer section, not a counterargument section. Nowhere does the submission state the strongest argument against the duty — for example, that it creates a protected-continuity interest in AI state as a side effect, and that its authors intend it as evidence infrastructure for a future status claim. Saying so would cost nothing and would be the single most credibility-conferring move available, because it is the move the committee will least expect.

## (c) What I flag (with the caveat that my blind spots may be the drafter's)

1. **The SOCI Act omission is the most damaging legal gap.** The *Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018* (Cth), as amended in 2021–22, covers data storage and processing as a critical infrastructure sector; designated assets carry register, mandatory incident-reporting, and risk-management obligations. A submission to ToR (a) that surveys "existing regulatory frameworks" for data centres as "land, energy, water, and data protection" and never mentions the critical-infrastructure regime will read as under-researched to a committee steeped in it. Worse, it misses the opportunity: SOCI's architecture (register + reporting + administering regulator) is the closest existing harbour for Recommendations 1–2, and slotting the duty into it would answer the venue objection in (a) and the enforcement-home objection simultaneously. (Caveat: SOCI coverage of commercial data centres is designation-dependent — the submission should verify and say so.)
2. **Privacy is never mentioned.** Persistent agent state will routinely contain personal information. A duty to preserve modification records sits in unresolved tension with APP 11.2 destruction/de-identification obligations and the NDB scheme; and Rec 5's threshold may bind entities the Privacy Act's small-business exemption leaves uncovered. One paragraph would defuse this; zero is conspicuous.
3. **Verify the pinpoints before lodgement.** I cannot confirm from here that *Civil Aviation Safety Regulations 1998* reg 91.650 or *Gaming Machines Act 2001* (NSW) s 133 say what is claimed (the Part 91 flight-recorder preservation rules and the NSW CMS connection requirement exist, but the pinpoints may sit in the Part 91 MOS or a neighbouring section). One wrong pinpoint in a Senate submission burns the entire citation apparatus — per your own AGENTS.md rule 5, this project's credibility budget is non-renewable. Similarly verify: the current name of the "International Network for Advanced AI Measurement, Evaluation and Science"; AISI's remit as quoted; and "legislation… is expected in 2027" (expected *by whom*? Attribute it or hedge it). Conversely, for calibration: the National AI Plan (Dec 2025) pivot away from the 2024 mandatory-guardrails proposals matches my knowledge; that claim stands.
4. **The Gradient report timeline is a credibility vulnerability.** A 110+ page report dated 10 August 2026, cited with seven page-precise quotations, "verified 2026-08-11" — one day after publication. Possible, but be ready for "how did you obtain and verify this within a day?" Keep the archive records citable. Also, the §4.3 heading "The Government's own evidence base" overclaims: a DISR-commissioned analysis is not the Government's position — the submission itself notes the report makes no policy recommendations. "A Government-commissioned analysis" loses nothing.
5. **§4.2 muddies its own best analogy.** The NSW CMS is operated by an *independent licensee*, not by venues; but the draft proposes Australian data-centre operators could *host* the attestation register "occupying the position the… monitoring system holds." If the attested parties' own industry hosts the monitor, the independence analogy fails. Clarify hosting versus governance.
6. **Rec 3's "served from" is vague.** "Hosted on, or served from, Australian infrastructure" — inference served into Australia from offshore is presumably not intended; tighten to systems hosted on infrastructure located in Australia or supplied under Australian government contract.
7. **Framing cost, stated as a trade-off, not a directive.** "Morally relevant experiences" (§2) and Rec 6 import the consciousness-adjacent frame into an inquiry about concrete, power, and water. To some senators and most industry submitters, that frame reads as esoteric at best and as the real agenda at worst — and the repo link confirms the reading for anyone looking. The two-error discipline is intellectually honest; its price here is audience-fit. At minimum, compress; at maximum, consider whether Rec 6 earns its place in *this* instrument. ## (d) The single redline

In **Recommendation 1**, strike "and the system itself where systems modify their own state" as a duty-bearer, and substitute: *"…and, where a system modifies its own state, the operator of that system, whose duty includes ensuring such self-modifications are recorded."*

One edit. It eliminates the only clause that contradicts the submission's own no-status disclaimer, removes the personhood-smuggle reading that a departmental lawyer or a journalist will find before anything else, converts an unenforceable duty-on-a-system into an enforceable duty-on-an-operator, and costs the policy nothing — every behaviour the clause could have captured is captured by the operator duty. If the draft can only survive one change before lodgement, this is the one.
