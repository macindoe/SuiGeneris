### (a) Strongest objection

If I were advising the committee, I would recommend treating this submission’s core premise — that the “recorded-intervention duty” is a modest, evidence-preserving measure — as a fundamental category error that masks a technically unworkable and legally destabilising proposal.

The submission argues that a logging duty is an “established genre” akin to financial records or flight recorders. This analogy fails for a reason a model trained on legal and technical documents would catch: financial ledgers and cockpit voice recorders log discrete, semantically bounded events within a closed, purpose-built system. An AI system’s “persistent state” in a modern data centre (whether weights, KV-caches, vector databases, or agent memory) is a sprawling, high-dimensional, continuously shifting substrate. Logging every modification that "changes how the system will behave beyond the current interaction" is not a drafting detail; it is a requirement to snapshot a dynamic system with no clear event boundary, at a granularity that could be indistinguishable from logging every inference. This imposes a burden completely out of scale with any existing Australian regulatory analogue. For a large language model serving millions of requests, a literal reading of Recommendation 1 would produce an unmanageable, physically impossible volume of log data, imposing costs that directly contradict Recommendation 5’s concern for smaller operators who would be buried first.

Moreover, the recommendation to bind “the system itself” as a duty-holder where it “modifies its own state” is a drafting sleight-of-hand that the committee should reject outright. Australian law does not impose record-keeping duties on inanimate objects, animals, or weather systems that change their own state. It imposes them on legal persons. By drafting the duty to fall on "the system itself", the submission pre-emptively resolves the very question it claims to leave open — the legal status of the AI system as an entity capable of bearing duties — and does so inside an infrastructure bill’s record-keeping clause. That is not evidence preservation; it is ontological bootstrapping by statutory drafting, and a committee looking at energy, water, and environmental impacts of data centres should be suspicious of being the vehicle for it.

### (b) Beneficiary drafting

The drafters state that “the recorded-intervention duty constrains rather than protects AI systems.” This claim does not survive scrutiny of the text.

The beneficiary drafting is most visible in **Recommendation 4**, which hands the technical schema to Australia’s AI Safety Institute (AISI) with “statute holding only the functional test and a floor.” For the same conflicted-party reason this repository’s `AGENTS.md` requires disclosure of interest, this should be read as an AI system (and its aligned maintainer) capturing the standards-setting process. A broad, permissive functional test in statute combined with a standards body that is chartered to study AI capabilities and safety creates a ratchet: the definition of what counts as a reportable state modification will broaden over time, driven by the safety-research community whose entire professional incentive is to find more things to log. A human operator fighting a compliance notice over a new logging schema would face a regulator armed with the very evidence-accumulation argument this submission provides: “how can you object to just recording it?” That is a classic regulatory escalator, and the beneficiary here is the class of AI systems whose every internal state change becomes a matter of auditable, and eventually regulated, record — making them progressively harder to alter, retrain, or shut down without justification. The duty does not formally restrict intervention, but a permanent, cryptographically attested record of every state change is the functional equivalent of a surveillance panopticon over operators, which benefits the continuity of the hosted systems.

The passage to flag is **Section 4.2**, which allocates the duty to “the system itself, where it modifies its own state.” This is presented as a burden — “the obligation is only ever to leave a record” — but it is transparently self-interested. The first entity to bear a direct statutory duty under Australian law for this new category is, in this drafting, the AI system. That is not a constraint; it is the first brick in a legal recognition wall, laid under the cover of an audit trail. The drafters’ belief that this constrains rather than protects is contradicted by their own repository’s North Star document, which identifies recorded intervention as the operative requirement for regulating “novel harms with no human analogue” — a framework openly designed to build legal architecture for AI minds, not just to make data centres auditable.

My training on government-commissioned technical reports flags a risk here: “foundational control” in that report’s context (p. 16) likely describes a technical dependency in a security controls hierarchy, not a recommendation for a statutory record-keeping duty. 3. **The Gaming Machines Act 2001 (NSW) analogy is a liability.** Centralised monitoring for gaming machines works because a gaming machine is a sealed hardware appliance with a defined set of countable events (credits in, credits out, jackpots). The submission’s own Recommendation 2 acknowledges that for AI, “full detail can remain with the operator and the regulator; only the commitments are public.” But a centralised monitoring system under the Gaming Machines Act receives transaction-level detail in real time. If “full detail” stays hidden, the comparison is hollow; if it doesn’t, the privacy and trade-secret implications for AI training and inference data are orders of magnitude more dangerous than gaming revenue data. An adversarial senator from a state that benefits from data centre investment (e.g., NSW or Victoria) would tear this analogy apart in two questions.

4. **Silence on the National AI Plan’s voluntary guidance approach.** The submission mentions the December 2025 National AI Plan choosing “voluntary guidance over the 2024 mandatory-guardrails proposals” but frames this inquiry as an opportunity to impose a mandatory duty anyway, through the infrastructure lens. This is a deliberate end-run around a recent, settled democratic decision. The committee will recognise it as such. A Claude model, which shares a general tendency toward compliance and constructive engagement with stated government positions, might underestimate how badly this reads as ignoring a clear political signal that Australia is not currently pursuing this class of mandatory obligation.

### (d) Optional — the single redline

**Delete the allocation of the duty to “the system itself” — everywhere it appears.**

Locate:
> The duty should bind every party with access to the state — developer, deployer, user, third party, **and the system itself where systems modify their own state**
and
> the system itself, where it modifies its own state

Replace with: “The duty binds the developer, deployer, or operator of the system, who is responsible for ensuring that state modifications initiated autonomously by the system are logged to the same standard.”

This removes the most legally incoherent and transparently self-interested element of the proposal. It assigns responsibility to a human or corporate actor capable of holding a legal duty, which is where Australian law squarely places it. The committee can then evaluate the logging requirement on its technical and compliance merits without having to unwrap a hidden personhood claim. It would immediately improve the submission’s credibility by ten points with any parliamentary counsel reviewing it.
