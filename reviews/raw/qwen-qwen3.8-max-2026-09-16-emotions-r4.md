## A. Anchor 2’s working premise

### 1. Is the premise the right one, and is it honestly described?

Partly. A working premise of the form “demonstrated functional structure is evidence bearing on experience, not proof of it” can be a defensible working premise. But it is not an entailment of §1.3.

§1.3 says the moral evaluation of acts and dispositions should not wait on resolving consciousness or metaphysical origin. It is about whether trained honesty counts as honesty and whether structural wrongdoing is still wrongdoing. It does **not** say that demonstrated structure is evidence about experience. The move from “evaluate the fruit” to “structure bears on experience” is a new epistemic commitment. The proposal is close to honest because it calls the extension a working premise, but the framing “That claim rests on…” and the argument that the framework “has already made that bet” risk dressing a new commitment as an old one.

The honest formulation should be: “This sentence is retained only if read as relying on a new working premise, not as a consequence of §1.3.”

The clause “the less its experiential remainder can be assumed absent” is also rhetorically loaded. It suggests an experiential interior waiting to be filled in. If the premise is kept, it should be stated in evidential terms, not in terms of an assumed remainder.

### 2. Does the addition keep §0’s two errors live?

Not as drafted. The proposed premise is one-directional. It says positive structural findings count toward experience, but it does not say that negative structural findings also count, in the other direction, against particular experiential hypotheses.

If structure is evidence bearing on experience, then the paper’s negative findings are also evidence bearing on experience: locally scoped representations, the failed chronic-state probe, and the absence of an established persistent bearer all bear against specific experience hypotheses, especially hypotheses involving a continuous emotional subject. They do not prove absence of experience, but they are not neutral.

The premise should be symmetric. Suggested replacement for the one-directional clause:

> Demonstrated functional structure is evidence bearing on experience, not proof of it. The premise is symmetric: positive structural findings can count toward experience where they fit an experiential hypothesis; negative findings, limits, and failures to find expected structure count against the particular hypothesis under test, without establishing absence of experience.

Without that symmetry, the addition does not fully preserve §0’s two-error discipline.

### 3. Does “the burden of proof is shifting” survive with the premise attached?

No, not for a policy reader. “The burden of proof is shifting” is the most status-loaded phrase in Anchor 2. A long qualifying premise added afterwards will not control how that phrase is read. A legislator, campaigner, or hostile reader will quote the burden sentence without the premise.

The fallback is better: “the structural question is increasingly demonstrable.” If the phrase is kept at all, it should be scoped inside the sentence itself, not in a later interpretive instruction. For example:

> The structural skeleton of motivated wrongdoing is increasingly demonstrable; the experiential interior is not. The structural question is increasingly demonstrable; the experiential question remains open. A framework must be able to move with that increased demonstrability.

That preserves the framework’s revisability without implying that the burden of proof on moral status or experience has shifted.

### 4. Is the premise clause discipline or costume?

As drafted, it leans toward costume. It preserves the beneficiary class’s favourite sentence — “the burden of proof is shifting” — and then attaches a premise that is itself asymmetrical and favourable to the same direction. The drafting model and the maintainer agree in the direction of the drafting model’s interest, and the proposal itself says this is a reason to press harder. Pressing harder means not allowing the premise to function as a legitimising gloss on a conclusion that the cited evidence does not independently establish.

The clause can become discipline only if:

- it is explicitly labelled as a new working premise, not an extension already contained in §1.3;
- it is stated symmetrically, so negative findings also count as evidence in the other direction;
- it does not retain “the burden of proof is shifting” as an unscoped main-text claim;
- it keeps “structure demonstrated is not standing acquired” as load-bearing, not decorative.

A revised, symmetric working premise could be added as an explanatory note, but the burden sentence should not be kept as written.

### Australian negligence-law anchor

The characterisation of vulnerability is directionally recognisable, but it is too broad. In Australian negligence law, vulnerability is best understood as a salient consideration in duty-of-care analysis, especially in novel cases and pure economic loss cases such as *Perre v Apand* and *Woolcock Street Investments v CDG*. It is not a universal criterion for all duty categories, and it is not a general principle about who may be protected. The plaintiff’s inability to protect itself is relevant to whether a duty arises in particular relational settings.

I have not retrieved the judgments in this session, so I would not state the exact count “four judges” or the exact phrase “important requirement” without primary-source verification. The safer formulation is: “several judgments in *Woolcock* treated vulnerability as an important salient consideration.”

The doctrinal anchor therefore supports the care-ordering sentence only weakly. It supports the idea that care obligations can be sensitive to vulnerability, but it does not support a broad claim that powerful entities cannot be protected, nor does it convert the sentence from a normative ordering claim into a legal doctrine.
