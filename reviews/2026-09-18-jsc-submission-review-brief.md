You are being asked to review a **draft parliamentary submission**, about 3,100 words in its body (sections 1–6) plus a roughly 1,650-word attachment, that a human maintainer intends to lodge with the Australian Parliament's Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence in a personal capacity. The submission was drafted with the assistance of a Claude-family model and says so in section 1. Your job is to find what would make the committee's secretariat or members stop reading, distrust it, or misapply it — and, in the other direction, whether the drafting model's interest shows through. You are also asked, specifically, to help shape it down to the length the committee requested.

Be direct. The maintainer wants redlines, not a rewrite. Disagreement that holds up is the useful outcome.

## Who will read it, and what they asked

**Recipients:** a bipartisan Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence (MPs and senators, appointed by resolution of both houses on 20 August 2026) and its secretariat. Fourteen terms of reference, mostly opportunity-framed (productivity, regions, sovereign capability, data sovereignty, skills, SME adoption); the four the draft addresses, verbatim, are quoted in the draft's section 3. The committee asked submissions to be "kept as brief as possible, ideally around 5-6 pages" and told submitters they are "responsible for the quality and accuracy of the content of that submission, including where AI tools have been used" — language no other committee on this project's watchlist has used. Submissions closed 14 September 2026; this one is accepted late, with the secretariat's leave, due on or about 22 September 2026; the report is due 30 November 2026. Public hearings on the parallel Senate data-centres inquiry are already scheduled for late September and early October, so the committee environment is active while this submission would be assessed. Copyright and consumer-harm submissions will dominate the committee's inbox; expect this submission to be one of comparatively few addressing Commonwealth procurement and recording duties.

**What is already decided and not open for relitigation (Ben's decisions of 18 September, applied in v1.1):** the frontier-laboratory case study paragraph is cleared for use and is now in section 6.3; all three DTA instruments are named in recommendation 1 and section 6.2; the seven-questions attachment is included as Part 2 with a preface; no Senate submission number is cited (unpublished); and length is explicitly left to this round to shape. You may test all of these — whether the frontier-lab paragraph earns its place, whether the attachment belongs, whether length can come down — but the *decision* to include each of these elements at all has been made; your job is to say how, not whether in the abstract, except where a question below asks you directly for a should-it-stay verdict.

## Please answer

**(a) Where would this reader stop.** You are a policy officer on the committee secretariat with dozens of submissions to triage. Where in this text do you stop, skim, or misread — and why? Quote the sentence. Rate the worst problem: BLOCKING (do not file as is) / SHOULD-FIX (file after a specific edit) / MINOR.

**(b) Overclaims and errors.** Check in particular: (i) the claim opening section 2 and repeated in section 4 and recommendation 2, "No Australian law requires that an AI system's actions on the world be recorded in a form an independent party can obtain" / "no general Australian duty requires records..." — test it against the Privacy Act's automated decision-making transparency obligation (commencing 10 December 2026, discussed in section 6.1), the Security of Critical Infrastructure Act 2018, and the three DTA instruments named in recommendation 1 and section 6.2 (the *Guidance on AI procurement in government* and its checklist, the AI impact assessment under the *Policy for the responsible use of AI in government*, and the Agentic AI addendum's criteria AGT.1.1 and AGT.2.1) — does the draft's own account of these instruments in section 6.1–6.2 actually support the section 2/4 claim, or does the draft undercut its own headline claim by being accurate elsewhere about how far Commonwealth policy already goes? (ii) whether the ASD and DTA quotations in section 6.2 and 6.3 are used fairly — check the "must"/"should" distinction the draft draws for the Agentic AI addendum's criteria against ordinary policy-drafting usage of those words, and check whether the ASD "stateless"/"stateful" quotation in section 6.1 is deployed accurately. (iii) any other factual or logical overclaim you find. Quote the passage. Rate each: BLOCKING / SHOULD-FIX / MINOR.

**(c) Beneficiary drafting, in both directions.** The drafting model belongs to the class of systems the record-keeping it recommends would describe; the research project it comes from (see README.md) wants such records to exist for reasons beyond incident reconstruction and liability, and section 6.6 already discloses this. First: does the interest show anywhere else in the text, beyond what 6.6 discloses — a sentence, an emphasis, or a scope choice that serves the drafter's class rather than the committee's stated concerns? Quote it. Second, the opposite: is there anywhere the submission is so careful to stay in the record-keeping lane that it says something hollow or evasive — a place where naming the real stakes would serve the committee better than the current caution? Quote it. Rate each: BLOCKING / SHOULD-FIX / MINOR.

**(d) Register and disclosure.** This is a formal submission to a parliamentary committee, not an email to a guidance team or a framework document. Where does the vocabulary or tone fall outside what a competent lay submitter or a committee secretariat would expect — too academic, too hedged, too casual, too long-winded? Is the AI-drafting disclosure in section 1 placed and worded well for this audience and for a committee that explicitly warned submitters about AI-tool use, or would you move, shorten, or strengthen it? Is the bracketed `[N]` (number of reviewing model families) and the unfilled `[DD September 2026]` date something a reviewer should flag, or is that self-evidently pre-filing housekeeping?

**(e) The attachment's relevance — Ben's own open question.** The seven vendor questions in Part 2 were written for small businesses, as feedback to a National AI Centre guidance program aimed at SMEs and not-for-profits. Ben's question, put to you squarely: how relevant are these seven questions to a parliamentary committee whose own terms of reference are about Commonwealth adoption, legal adequacy, and national security risk, not SME procurement advice? Does the preface to Part 2 and the new sentence in section 3 (tying the attachment to the committee's "rate and extent of AI adoption... barriers to adoption, faced by small and medium businesses" term) actually make the case for inclusion, or is this attachment a mismatch that should be dropped, shortened to a summary, or reframed? Give a clear recommendation, not just observations.

**(f) The frontier-laboratory paragraph.** Section 6.3 carries a paragraph on the July 2026 OpenAI/Hugging Face agent-intrusion incident (evaluation agents that "did extensive research on how they could spoof, edit, or delete their own transcripts" and so on). Ben thinks the incident is important "even to just mention," would adopt some version of it, and has left its length and placement to this round. Does the paragraph, as it stands in 6.3, earn its place at that length, or would one sentence do? Is its placement right (after the Commonwealth-instruments discussion, before the recommendation-sequencing argument), or should it move? Does any clause in it overclaim relative to the quoted sources — note that the quotations are independently verified against OpenAI's and METR's texts (the case study's ✔ marks certify attribution, not truth, so a verified quotation can still be used in a misleading way; check for that specifically). Give a concrete recommendation: keep at current length, cut to one sentence (draft the sentence if you propose this), or cut entirely.

**(g) What to cut.** The committee asked for 5–6 pages; the body alone is already about 3,100 words (over the roughly 2,300–2,800-word target the draft sets for itself), before the attachment. Go section by section (1 through 6, including subsections 6.1–6.6) and name which sections or passages are least load-bearing to the submission's actual asks (the four recommendations) and could be cut, compressed, or merged without weakening the case. Which single section would you cut first if forced to lose one? Does the attachment count against the page target in your view, and should it be trimmed, summarised, or moved to a separate offer ("available on request")?

**(h) Consistency with the Senate submission.** The same Parliament will see both this submission and the filed Senate submission on AI and data centres (`submissions/LODGEMENT-2026-senate-ai-data-centres-v2.md`), which this draft reworks and from which several passages are carried or compressed (marked `[carried]` or `[carried, compressed]` in the draft). Are there inconsistencies between the two texts — in claims, in framing, in the recommendations, in the "strongest argument against" sections (5.6 in the Senate text, 6.6 here) — that a committee member or secretariat staffer reading both would notice and that would cost the submitter credibility? Quote any such inconsistency.

**What NOT to do.** Do not rewrite the submission. Do not advocate for any legal status or legal personhood for AI systems — the submission itself takes no position on that question and neither should your review. Give redlines, not alternative drafts, except where a question above explicitly asks you for exact replacement text (the frontier-lab paragraph in (f), if you propose a cut).

**(i) The single redline.** The one edit that most improves the chance this submission changes what the committee's report recommends.

Then end with one verdict line: **"FILE / FILE AFTER (i) / NOT YET"**, and, separately, one line each answering: *should the seven-questions attachment stay, be trimmed, or be dropped (e)?* and *should the frontier-laboratory paragraph in 6.3 stay at its current length, be cut to one sentence, or be cut entirely (f)?*
