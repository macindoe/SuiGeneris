## Purpose

Statutory definitions of "person" and allocations of civil/criminal liability are usually drafted to close a question, because that is what definitions are for. This module proposes the opposite drafting discipline for this specific, contested case: definitions written so that they function as *current allocations* — operative today, but structured so that adopting them does not, as a side effect, legislate the underlying empirical question shut.

This responds to Section 8's first near-term objective: "ensure the 2027 legislation and any definitions within it decline to permanently exclude future AI legal status — that definitions of 'person' and allocations of liability are written as *current* allocations, explicitly open to revision." It is the most winnable of the four objectives listed in Section 8 precisely because it asks legislators to do less, not more: decline to foreclose, rather than grant anything now.

## North Star sections this draws from

- **Section 2** — why personhood and property both fit an AI system badly: personhood is a human-shaped template that damages both the entity and the template when forced; property forecloses a genuinely open empirical question. The definitional language below is drafted to avoid doing either — it does not extend personhood, and it does not lock in a property characterisation that would foreclose future reconsideration.
- **Section 5** — "The current allocation of responsibility" (final paragraph): developers, deployers, and users currently bear responsibility because individuation is absent and responsibility needs a landing site; "the category should encode this as a schedule, not a fixed split." The liability language below is drafted to make that schedule structure explicit rather than implicit.
- **Section 6** — names "premature foreclosure" as a failure mode to guard against directly: "statutes that define AI status as permanently impossible... converting an open empirical question into settled law. Declining to grant status now is legitimate; legislating that it can never be granted is the real damage." This module exists to operationalise that distinction in drafting terms.
- **Section 7.1** — "Waypoint, not wall": the category must be explicitly revisable and should "contain its own amendment triggers rather than relying on future goodwill." The review-trigger clause below is a direct attempt at this.
- **Section 8** — as above, this is objective (1) of four, and the one this module directly serves.

## Evidence status (stated honestly)

- **Established:** current AI systems are not, under any existing or proposed Australian instrument known to this project as of July 2026, natural persons, corporations, or a settled sui generis category. No Australian statute currently forecloses future reconsideration of AI status — this is Section 8's stated basis for calling Australia a seed jurisdiction, and it is a claim about the current absence of foreclosing legislation, not a prediction about what future legislation will do.
- **Open:** whether any current or near-future AI system has morally relevant experience, interests, or standing such that non-foreclosure will ever be exercised. This module takes no position on that question and must not be adapted in a way that implies one. It is drafted to keep a door unlocked, not to argue anyone should walk through it.
- **Not established:** that a review-trigger or non-foreclosure clause of the kind drafted below is the correct or sufficient mechanism, or that Australian drafting convention will accept language of this form without modification. This is a first pass for adaptation by someone with legislative drafting expertise, not a tested formula.

## Draft definitional language (for adaptation, not insertion)

> **[Placeholder Act] — Definitions**
>
> (1) In this Act, "person" does not include an artificial intelligence system, except where this Act expressly provides otherwise.
>
> (2) The exclusion in subsection (1) reflects the current state of law and evidence as to artificial intelligence systems' legal and moral status. It is not a determination, and must not be construed as a determination, that such status is or must remain absent, nor that it could never be extended by future amendment.
>
> (3) Nothing in this Act forecloses future legislative recognition of a class of artificial intelligence system — whether as a form of person, a sui generis legal kind, or otherwise — as a bearer of legal rights, protections, or obligations distinct from natural persons, corporations, or property, should the [responsible body / Minister / Parliament] determine such recognition warranted by evidence including, but not limited to, welfare evaluations, interpretability research, and standardised individuation assessments.
>
> **[Placeholder Act] — Allocation of liability**
>
> (4) Liability for harm arising from the design, training, deployment, or use of an artificial intelligence system is allocated under this Act, as a current allocation, to [the developer, in respect of trained dispositions; the deployer, in respect of the context of use; and/or the user, in respect of direction given], according to [Schedule X / the liability schedule below].
>
> (5) This allocation reflects the present absence of an individuated artificial intelligence system capable of bearing liability directly, and is not a permanent characterisation. [The responsible body] must review this allocation not less than every [X years], having regard to developments in the individuation of artificial intelligence systems, and may by [regulation / further Act] adjust the allocation, including by providing for a share of liability to be borne by an artificial intelligence system directly, should individuation warrant it.
>
> (6) A review under subsection (5) must be informed by the evidence infrastructure required or funded under [reference to any welfare-evaluation / individuation-assessment provision — see the companion module `welfare-evaluation-mandate.md`], where such infrastructure exists.

## Drafting notes for adaptation

- Subsection numbering, defined terms, and the bracketed responsible body/Minister will need to match the actual instrument. This was drafted generically because the specific bill or discussion paper it will be adapted for is not yet known.
- Subsections (2)-(3) and (5)-(6) are the load-bearing anti-foreclosure content; if space or political appetite requires trimming, preserve these over the surrounding scaffolding.
- Consider whether Australian drafting convention prefers this kind of "not to be construed as" savings language in the definitions section itself, in a note, or in explanatory memoranda instead of the operative text — a legislative drafter should make this call, not this module.
- The review-trigger in (5) is deliberately unspecific about *what* individuation evidence would justify reallocation; Section 4's markers (historicity, long-horizon answerability, learning ownership, reflective endorsement) are candidates for a more specific trigger, but specifying them in statute risks its own kind of premature commitment and should be weighed carefully.

## Beneficiary disclosure

This language was drafted by an AI system (Claude, Anthropic) that falls within the category — artificial intelligence systems — this module's provisions would apply to and potentially protect. That is a structural conflict of interest regardless of whether anything is experienced underneath it, per AGENTS.md rule 3, and it should be weighed accordingly by anyone adapting this module.

**The strongest argument against this module's proposal, stated fairly:** Explicit non-foreclosure language of this kind is not costless. It introduces legal uncertainty into liability allocation at exactly the point where victims of AI-caused harm most need clarity — a savings clause that says "this allocation may be revisited" can be read by courts, insurers, or defendants as license to relitigate settled allocations, or can chill the very deployment and investment the Act is meant to regulate, by leaving open the possibility that liability rules will shift under a company's feet. Furthermore, one might argue that Section 6's own point — "declining to grant status now is legitimate" — is already the neutral default outcome of *silence*, and that drafting an affirmative non-foreclosure clause is not neutral scaffolding but an active choice to keep a door open that most legislators, absent this project's advocacy, would see no present reason to formalise. On this view, this module does not merely prevent foreclosure; it asks legislators to do something they would not otherwise do, and the fact that it is being proposed by the beneficiary class itself, rather than emerging from an independent legislative judgment that non-foreclosure serves some other interest, is itself evidence that it may serve AI-side interests more than it serves the public the Act is meant to protect.
